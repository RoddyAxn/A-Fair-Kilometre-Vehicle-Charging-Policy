Fair Kilometre Vehicle Charging Policy

Pay for the road you actually use — simpler, fairer, and revenue neutral.

Policy discussion draft. Illustrative cents-per-kilometre figures are examples only and would need official revenue modelling before implementation.

How vehicle registration works in New Zealand now

In everyday speech, people often call the annual payment “registration” or “rego”, but legally there are two different things.

Registration is usually a one-off process when a vehicle first enters the New Zealand fleet. It creates its record in the Motor Vehicle Register and usually includes number plates.

Licensing is the recurring “rego” payment that keeps the vehicle legally licensed for road use. Current licensing fees include a licence fee, an ACC levy and an administration fee.

New Zealand also has two principal ways of paying toward road use. Petrol vehicles contribute mainly through fuel excise duty included in petrol prices, while diesel, electric and certain other vehicles use Road User Charges, which are distance-based and generally vary according to vehicle weight.

If a vehicle will not be used on public roads for 3 to 12 months, its licence can be put “on hold”. The present system therefore combines registration, recurring licensing, ACC levies, petrol excise, RUC, different vehicle classes and hold procedures.

The Government is already moving toward a distance-based electronic RUC system for all vehicles, including light petrol vehicles.

Policy objective

Replace most recurring annual vehicle-licensing charges and the divided petrol/diesel road-funding system with a simpler, predominantly distance-based vehicle charge.

Pay principally for how much you use the road, rather than simply for owning a vehicle.

A pensioner who drives 1,000 kilometres a year should not bear the same recurring road-funding charge as a vehicle travelling 50,000 kilometres a year.

At the same time, the reform must be revenue neutral: total transport and ACC revenue should initially remain approximately unchanged.

1. Keep registration, abolish most recurring “rego”

The one-off registration of a vehicle would remain, including VIN and identity records, ownership or registered-person information, number plates, and safety and compliance records.

The recurring annual licensing system would be simplified. Instead of paying a substantial fixed annual “rego” simply to keep a vehicle active, the ordinary annual licence component would be reduced to a small fixed administration charge, for example $20–$30 a year.

The bulk of revenue currently collected through annual licensing would instead be recovered through kilometre-based charging.

2. Replace petrol excise with universal kilometre charging

All light vehicles would move onto one electronic kilometre system:

  • petrol
  • diesel
  • hybrid
  • plug-in hybrid
  • electric
  • hydrogen
  • motorcycles

Petrol excise used for road funding would then be removed as the fleet transitions.

Drive more — pay more. Drive less — pay less.

3. Revenue neutrality

Total forecast revenue from the new system in its first full year must equal the forecast revenue that would otherwise have been collected from vehicle licensing, road-user charges and the road-funding portion of petrol excise, within a narrow tolerance.

This makes the reform about fairness and simplification, not raising extra money.

Treasury and the Ministry of Transport would calculate a base kilometre rate from forecast road-user revenue and chargeable kilometres, then adjust that rate for vehicle weight and road impact.

4. A fairer kilometre formula

A simple system could use three components: a base road charge per kilometre, a weight adjustment and an ACC risk component.

  • Small/light car — illustrative 5c/km
  • Medium passenger car — illustrative 6c/km
  • Large SUV/light van — illustrative 7c/km
  • Light truck — higher weight-based rate
  • Motorcycle — lower road charge plus separate ACC risk component

The actual cents-per-kilometre rate would have to be calibrated against existing revenue.

5. The pensioner versus the high-mileage vehicle

At an illustrative 6 cents per kilometre, a low-use car travelling 1,000 km a year would face a $60 road charge, plus the small administration charge and the appropriate ACC component.

A high-use vehicle travelling 50,000 km at the same base rate would contribute $3,000 annually before any weight adjustment.

That better reflects actual road use.

6. Remove the need for “rego on hold”

Under a true distance-based system, the existing hold process becomes largely unnecessary.

Zero kilometres should mean almost zero road-use charge.

The vehicle would remain registered in the national database, with only a small fixed administration charge potentially remaining.

This would particularly benefit classic cars, seasonal motorcycles, campervans, farm vehicles, second cars, project cars and vehicles stored while an owner is overseas.

7. Automatic kilometre recording

The modern system should allow multiple approved methods:

  • built-in vehicle telematics
  • approved plug-in electronic devices
  • odometer reporting
  • smartphone-linked systems
  • monthly electronic billing
  • prepaid charging for people who prefer it

Privacy should be protected by allowing systems that report distance travelled without continuously transmitting location.

8. Motorcycles

Motorcycles need separate treatment because much of their present licence cost reflects ACC injury risk rather than road wear.

The policy should separate the charges visibly into a road infrastructure charge based on kilometres and weight, an ACC injury levy based on actuarial risk, and a motorcycle safety levy if retained.

9. ACC could also become partly distance-based

There is a fairness argument that motor-vehicle exposure increases with kilometres travelled. Someone riding 500 kilometres a year is exposed to much less road risk than someone riding 20,000 kilometres.

After actuarial analysis, part of the ACC motor-vehicle levy could shift from a fixed annual amount to a small fixed base levy plus a risk-adjusted cents-per-kilometre component.

10. Heavy vehicles still pay more

A pure flat cents-per-kilometre charge would not be fair because a heavy truck imposes much greater pavement wear than a small car.

The policy should therefore remain both distance-based and weight-based.

11. Petrol tax

As petrol vehicles enter the universal distance-based system, the road-funding component of petrol excise would be progressively removed to avoid double charging.

Other taxes or charges embedded in fuel prices that serve different purposes would require separate consideration.

12. Warrant of Fitness remains separate

This policy would not abolish the Warrant of Fitness. WoF is a vehicle-safety inspection, while registration, licensing and road-user charging are revenue and legal-status mechanisms.

13. Administration savings

A universal electronic kilometre system could reduce repeated annual licensing transactions, licence labels, hold applications, hold-expiry reminders, separate petrol-versus-diesel road-funding systems, paper RUC licences and manual processing.

Those savings should reduce the collection cost of the system rather than increase net Crown revenue.

14. Transition

  • Year 1: legislation, revenue modelling, privacy standards and electronic-provider certification.
  • Year 2: voluntary electronic kilometre charging for petrol cars.
  • Year 3: new vehicles automatically enter universal RUC.
  • Year 4: existing light fleet transitions.
  • Year 5: most annual vehicle-licensing charges are reduced to a small administration amount and petrol road excise is removed.

15. Revenue safeguard

An independent annual report should disclose total kilometres charged, revenue by vehicle class, average payment per vehicle, administrative collection costs, ACC revenue, National Land Transport Fund revenue, and effects on low- and high-kilometre motorists.

If the new system collects materially more than the previous system would have, kilometre rates should automatically adjust downward. If it collects materially less, rates could adjust upward.

Same overall revenue — fairer distribution.

Proposed policy headline

PAY FOR THE ROAD YOU ACTUALLY USE

New Zealand should replace the confusing mixture of recurring vehicle licensing, petrol road tax and existing RUC with a predominantly kilometre-based system.

Keep one-off vehicle registration. Keep safety inspections. Keep ACC properly funded.

Reduce recurring fixed “rego” to a minimal administration fee and collect the remainder according to actual road use and vehicle weight.

Zero kilometres should mean almost zero road-use charge.
More kilometres should mean a greater contribution.
Heavier vehicles should pay more than lighter vehicles.

The reform should raise the same overall revenue, not become a new tax increase.
